01. Basic Chess Rules

The foundation of every chess game.

RULE 01

The Chessboard

A chessboard has 64 squares arranged in 8 rows and 8 columns. Each player begins with 16 pieces.

RULE 02

Initial Setup

The board is positioned with a light square on each player's right-hand corner. The queen starts on her own colour.

RULE 03

Taking Turns

White makes the first move. Players alternate turns, moving one piece per turn unless a special rule applies.

RULE 04

Capturing Pieces

A piece captures an opponent's piece by moving to its square, following that piece's legal movement rules.

RULE 05

Check

A king is in check when it is attacked by an opponent's piece. The player must respond to the check immediately.

RULE 06

Checkmate

Checkmate occurs when the king is in check and no legal move can remove the threat. The game ends immediately.

RULE 07

Stalemate

Stalemate occurs when a player has no legal move but their king is not in check. The game is drawn.

RULE 08

Illegal Moves

A move is illegal if it violates a piece's movement rules or leaves the player's own king in check.

02. Special Chess Rules

Important rules that every player should understand.

SPECIAL RULE 01
♔ ♖

Castling

Castling is a special move involving the king and a rook. The king moves two squares toward the rook, and the rook moves to the square next to the king.

Neither piece may have moved before; squares between them must be empty; the king cannot be in check, cross an attacked square or land on one.
SPECIAL RULE 02
♙

En Passant

If an opponent's pawn advances two squares from its starting position and lands beside your pawn, your pawn may capture it as though it moved only one square.

This capture must be made immediately on the next move. Otherwise the opportunity is lost.
SPECIAL RULE 03
♙ → ♕

Pawn Promotion

When a pawn reaches the last rank, it must be promoted to a queen, rook, bishop or knight of the same colour.

A player can have multiple queens. Promotion is not limited to pieces that have already been captured.
SPECIAL RULE 04
♙ ↟

Pawn's First Move

A pawn may move one or two squares forward on its first move, provided both squares are clear.

After its first move, it can normally move only one square forward.

03. Draw Rules

Understand when a chess game ends without a winner.

DRAW RULE 01

Draw by Agreement

Players may agree to a draw according to the rules and timing of the competition.

DRAW RULE 02

Threefold Repetition

A player may claim a draw when the same position occurs for the third time, with the same player to move and the same possible moves.

DRAW RULE 03

Fifty-Move Rule

A player may claim a draw if the last 50 moves by each player have been made without a pawn move or capture.

DRAW RULE 04

Insufficient Material

A game is drawn when no possible sequence of legal moves can lead to checkmate.

04. Tournament & Competition Rules

Important concepts for competitive chess.

COMPETITION 01

Touch-Move Rule

In over-the-board play, deliberately touching your own piece can require you to move it if a legal move is available. Touching an opponent's piece can also create an obligation to capture. Exceptions apply.

COMPETITION 02

Chess Clock

Players use a chess clock to manage their thinking time. After making a move, a player presses their clock to start the opponent's time.

COMPETITION 03

Flag Fall

A player who runs out of time may lose the game, unless the opponent cannot possibly checkmate by any legal sequence.

COMPETITION 04

Fair Play

Players must follow competition rules and must not receive unauthorized assistance during their games.

THE BEGINNING OF EVERY GAME

A Strong Opening Builds a Strong Game.

The opening is the first phase of a chess game. Your goal is to develop pieces, control the centre, protect your king and prepare a meaningful plan.

♞

01. Essential Opening Principles

Understand these ideas before memorizing opening moves.

01

Control the Centre

Fight for central squares, especially e4, d4, e5 and d5. Central control helps your pieces become more active.

02

Develop Your Pieces

Bring your knights and bishops into active positions. Avoid unnecessary moves with the same piece.

03

Protect Your King

Castling often helps your king reach safety and connects your rooks.

04

Connect Your Rooks

Develop your pieces and clear the back rank so your rooks can support one another.

05

Avoid Early Queen Adventures

Moving the queen too early can expose it to attacks and cost valuable development time.

06

Understand Your Plan

Do not memorize moves blindly. Understand what each move tries to achieve.

02. Opening Categories

Explore different opening styles and approaches.

♙

Open Games

Usually begin with 1.e4 e5. These often create open lines and active piece play.

♘

Semi-Open Games

White starts with 1.e4 and Black replies with a different move than 1...e5.

♗

Closed Games

Often start with 1.d4 d5 and may involve strategic pawn structures.

♖

Indian Defences

Black often develops with ...Nf6 and fianchetto or flexible central plans.

♕

Flank Openings

Begin with moves such as 1.c4, 1.Nf3 or 1.f4.

✦

Gambit Openings

Offer material, often a pawn, in exchange for development, initiative or attacking chances.

LEARN FROM EVERY MOVE

Every Mistake Is an Opportunity to Improve.

Strong players are not people who never make mistakes. They learn to recognize patterns, check threats and make better decisions over time.

⚠

01. Types of Chess Mistakes

Understand the different levels of move quality.

LOW SEVERITY
~

Inaccuracy

A move that is playable but less effective than a stronger alternative.

Example: Choosing a passive square for a piece.
MEDIUM SEVERITY
!

Mistake

A move that significantly worsens the position or misses an important opportunity.

Example: Allowing a tactical threat.
HIGH SEVERITY
??

Blunder

A serious error that can lose material, allow checkmate or badly damage the position.

Example: Leaving a queen undefended.
MISSED OPPORTUNITY
?!

Missed Chance

Failing to notice a strong move or tactical opportunity.

Example: Missing a winning fork.

02. Common Mistakes Every Player Should Avoid

Recognize these patterns before they damage your position.

01

Leaving Pieces Undefended

Moving a piece without checking whether an opponent can capture it.

Better approach: Before moving, check which pieces are attacked and which pieces are protected.
02

Ignoring Opponent Threats

Focusing only on your own plan while missing the opponent's immediate threats.

Better approach: Ask: What is my opponent threatening after this move?
03

Moving the Same Piece Too Many Times

Repeatedly moving one piece during the opening while other pieces remain undeveloped.

Better approach: Develop your minor pieces and work toward king safety.
04

Delaying King Safety

Leaving the king in the centre when the position makes it vulnerable.

Better approach: Consider castling when it is legal and strategically appropriate.
05

Making Unnecessary Pawn Moves

Creating weaknesses by moving too many pawns without a clear purpose.

Better approach: Move pawns with a plan and consider the squares they leave behind.
06

Capturing Without Calculating

Taking a piece without checking whether the capture leads to a disadvantage.

Better approach: Calculate the likely recapture and resulting position.
07

Ignoring Checks and Tactics

Missing forcing moves such as checks, captures and direct threats.

Better approach: Look for forcing moves for both players before committing.
08

Playing Too Quickly

Making decisions before understanding the position and possible responses.

Better approach: Use a consistent thinking routine and manage your clock.
09

Ignoring Piece Activity

Keeping pieces passive while the opponent develops active threats.

Better approach: Improve your least active piece and seek useful squares.
10

Not Reviewing Games

Repeating the same errors because previous games were never studied.

Better approach: Review important moments and record lessons from each game.

TACTICAL INTELLIGENCE CENTER

Think Smart. Find Strong Moves.

Learn tactical patterns, calculate combinations and recognize opportunities to gain an advantage.

PLAY WITH A PURPOSE

Don't Just Make a Move. Create a Problem for Your Opponent.

Smart chess is about recognizing threats, creating pressure, coordinating pieces and choosing moves that improve your position.

✦

01. Essential Tactical Patterns

Learn the patterns that help you win material and create threats.

♘
TACTIC 01

Fork

One piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time. The opponent may struggle to protect every target.

Typical idea: A knight attacks the king and queen simultaneously.
♗
TACTIC 02

Pin

A piece is restricted because moving it would expose a more valuable piece behind it.

Typical idea: A bishop pins a knight to the king.
♖
TACTIC 03

Skewer

A valuable piece is attacked first. When it moves away, a less valuable piece behind it may become exposed.

Typical idea: A rook attacks a king with a queen behind it.
✦
TACTIC 04

Discovered Attack

Moving one piece reveals an attack from another piece that was previously blocked.

Typical idea: A knight moves away and reveals a rook attack.
♕
TACTIC 05

Double Attack

A move creates two threats at once, forcing the opponent to make a difficult choice.

Typical idea: A move attacks a queen and threatens checkmate.
♜
TACTIC 06

Back-Rank Mate

A rook or queen checkmates a king trapped behind its own pieces on the back rank.

Typical idea: A rook reaches the back rank while escape squares are blocked.
♙
TACTIC 07

Pawn Promotion Threat

A passed pawn advances toward promotion, forcing the opponent to spend time stopping it.

Typical idea: A protected passed pawn creates a serious threat.
♞
TACTIC 08

Deflection

A defending piece is distracted or forced away from an important square or duty.

Typical idea: A defender is lured away from protecting the king.
♗
TACTIC 09

Decoy

An opponent's piece is tempted onto a square where it becomes vulnerable to a tactic.

Typical idea: A piece is drawn onto a dangerous square.
♕
TACTIC 10

Removing the Defender

Eliminate or exchange an important defending piece to make another target vulnerable.

Typical idea: Capture the only defender of a valuable piece.
♖
TACTIC 11

Overloading

A defending piece has too many responsibilities and cannot handle every threat.

Typical idea: One defender protects two important targets.
✧
TACTIC 12

Interference

A move blocks communication between enemy pieces or interrupts an important defensive line.

Typical idea: A piece blocks a rook from defending another piece.

02. Famous Checkmate Patterns

Learn how coordinated pieces can create checkmate threats.

PATTERN 01
♕

Back-Rank Checkmate

A rook or queen delivers mate along the back rank when the king has no safe escape square.

PATTERN 02
♘

Smothered Mate

A knight checkmates a king surrounded by its own pieces.

PATTERN 03
♗

Arabian Mate

A rook and knight coordinate to trap the king near a corner.

PATTERN 04
♕

Opera Mate

A famous mating pattern involving coordinated attacking pieces and restricted king movement.

PATTERN 05
♖

Two-Rook Mate

Two rooks coordinate to restrict the king and deliver checkmate along a rank or file.

PATTERN 06
♙

Queen and Pawn Mate

A queen and supporting pieces restrict the king's escape squares and create a mating net.

Use this thinking sequence to evaluate a position.

01

Look for Checks

Find legal checks that may create forcing opportunities.

02

Look for Captures

03

Look for Threats

04

05

Check King Safety

SMART GAME PLAN

How to Create Winning Opportunities

There is no guaranteed shortcut to winning a chess game. However, you can create practical opportunities by following sound chess principles.

01

Develop Quickly

Bring your pieces into useful positions and avoid wasting opening moves.

02

Control Important Squares

Fight for the centre and restrict your opponent's active options.

03

Find Weaknesses

Identify undefended pieces, weak pawns and vulnerable squares.

04

Coordinate Your Pieces

Make your pieces support one another instead of attacking alone.

05

Calculate Before Attacking

Check the opponent's best defensive response before committing.

06

Convert Your Advantage

When ahead, simplify carefully and avoid unnecessary risks.
